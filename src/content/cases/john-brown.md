## The case

On 16 October 1859 John Brown and a small band of followers seized the federal arsenal at Harpers Ferry, hoping to start an armed uprising against slavery. US Marines under Robert E. Lee captured the raiders two days later.

Virginia, not the federal government, put Brown on trial. The proceedings opened in Charles Town at the end of October, and Brown, who had been wounded in the fighting, was brought into court on a cot. Newspapers sent artists and reporters, and the trial drawings ran in the illustrated weeklies of the time.

The jury convicted him on all counts, and he was hanged a month later. The case is remembered as one of the events that deepened the divide between North and South before the Civil War.

## In the courtroom

The trial was extraordinarily fast: it opened nine days after the raid and the jury needed less than an hour. Brown lay on a cot because of his wounds, and when his Virginia lawyers were replaced by Northern ones, he tried at first to dismiss them. His defence was never really legal. He said he had come to help the enslaved, and he said he had not meant to harm anyone who did not fight him.

When the sentence was passed he told the court that if it was necessary for him to give up his life for the cause of the oppressed, he was ready. The speech was printed in Northern papers, and it made him a martyr.

## Aftermath

The artist of the sketch, David Hunter Strother, worked as "Porte Crayon" for Harper's Weekly and lived nearby. About 1,500 soldiers guarded the gallows on 2 December, and among the militia was the actor John Wilkes Booth. Within eighteen months the country was at war.
