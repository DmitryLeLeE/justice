## The case

The gold rush in Minas Gerais made the Portuguese Crown anxious to collect its royal fifth, the *quinto*. In 1719 it ordered the creation of smelting houses, *casas de fundição*, where all gold had to be melted into stamped bars and the fifth paid, so gold dust could no longer circulate. Colonists also resented the privileges of merchants born in Portugal. The governor, Dom Pedro de Almeida Portugal, Count of Assumar, was set on enforcing the rules.

## In the courtroom

On 28 June 1720 an armed crowd rose in Vila Rica, today Ouro Preto. Filipe dos Santos Freire, who spoke for the rebels, was arrested by the governor's forces at Cachoeira do Campo, where he was speaking outside a church. He was tried summarily, with no real account of the proceedings surviving. He was sentenced to be dragged through the town's streets, hanged and quartered, with the parts of his body displayed in several towns and his goods taken by the Crown. Accounts differ about the exact date and about details of the execution, and the popular story that he was torn apart by four horses is regarded by historians as a legend.

## Aftermath

The revolt ended in July 1720, and Minas Gerais was later made a separate captaincy, which tightened Portuguese control over the mines. Filipe dos Santos was remembered in Brazil as an early martyr of colonial resistance, and Antônio Parreiras painted the scene of his judgement.
