## The case

In 1998 a retired mayor of Hyde, Kathleen Grundy, died suddenly. Her daughter, a solicitor, found that a will that left almost all of her estate to her doctor, Harold Shipman, was a forgery, and told the police. Her body was exhumed and found to contain a lethal dose of diamorphine. Shipman, a family doctor in Hyde, near Manchester, was arrested in September 1998; investigators then examined the deaths of other patients and found a pattern.

## In the courtroom

The trial opened at Preston Crown Court in October 1999 and lasted three months. Shipman, tried on fifteen counts of murder, denied that he had killed anyone and never explained his actions. The prosecution showed that he had been the last person to see each of them alive, that their medical records had been altered to suggest illnesses they did not have, and that tests on several exhumed bodies found morphine. The jury found him guilty on all counts, and Mr Justice Forbes told him that life would mean life.

## Aftermath

Shipman was found hanged in his cell at Wakefield Prison in January 2004. A public inquiry led by Dame Janet Smith concluded that he had killed at least 218 patients between 1975 and 1998; the total is estimated at about 250. The case led to reforms in the way that deaths are certified and doctors are regulated in Britain.

## 18+ · The pattern

Most of Shipman's victims were elderly women, often found dead sitting in a chair or lying on a sofa, fully dressed, after he visited their home. He was the one who called the death in and signed the certificate. The inquiry identified 218 victims that it was sure or nearly sure of, and it believed that there were more. Why he did it was never explained, and he did not tell anyone.
