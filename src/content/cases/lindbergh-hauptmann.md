## The case

On 1 March 1932 the 20-month-old son of Charles Lindbergh, the first man to fly the Atlantic alone, was taken from the nursery of the family's house in East Amwell, New Jersey. A ransom note demanded $50,000, later raised, and a retired teacher, Dr John Condon, acted as go-between and handed the money over in a Bronx cemetery in April. On 12 May a truck driver found the child's body, with a fractured skull, near Mount Rose, a few miles from the house.

The break came in September 1934 when a gold-certificate bill from the ransom was traced to the car of Bruno Richard Hauptmann, a German immigrant carpenter in the Bronx. More of the money turned up in his garage.

## In the courtroom

The trial ran from 2 January to 13 February 1935 in Flemington, a town of fewer than 3,000 people that was overrun by reporters and spectators. Attorney General Wilentz argued that Hauptmann had built the ladder from a floorboard in his attic, wrote the ransom notes, and held the money. Handwriting experts linked his script to the notes, and Condon's address and telephone number were found written in his home. Edward Reilly's defence said the ransom money had been left with him by a friend who had died in Germany, and that the evidence had been planted or mishandled.

The jury found him guilty on all counts and he was sentenced to the electric chair.

## Aftermath

Appeals and a last-minute review by the governor failed, and Hauptmann was executed on 3 April 1936, still protesting his innocence. His widow Anna spent decades trying to clear his name and her lawsuits against the state in the 1980s were dismissed. The kidnapping led to the federal "Lindbergh Law" making interstate kidnapping a federal crime.
