## The case

By the end of 1648 Charles I had lost the Civil War, but he had also reopened it by making a secret agreement with the Scots. The army, which had seized power, expelled MPs who wanted to settle with the king in Pride's Purge in December, and the remaining Commons, the "Rump", passed an act creating a High Court of Justice to try him. The House of Lords rejected it, and the Commons declared that their own vote was enough.

## In the courtroom

The court sat in Westminster Hall from 20 January 1649, with John Bradshaw presiding and John Cooke as prosecutor. Only part of the 135 named commissioners sat. Charles refused to plead, insisting that no court had the right to try a king and that the body in front of him was not a lawful parliament. He was brought in three times and asked three times to answer, and each time he refused, so the court treated the charge as admitted.

On 27 January the court sentenced him to death. A story that has been repeated for centuries says that Charles was not allowed to speak after the sentence was read; the proceedings record that he asked to be heard and was refused.

## Aftermath

He was beheaded on a scaffold outside the Banqueting House on 30 January 1649. England was declared a republic. After the monarchy was restored in 1660, those of the judges who were still alive and had signed the death warrant were tried for regicide; ten were executed and others imprisoned.
