## The case

In 1616 the Church had declared the idea that the Sun is at rest and the Earth moves to be contrary to scripture, and Cardinal Robert Bellarmine told Galileo that he must not hold or defend it. In 1632 Galileo published the Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems, a conversation between three speakers in which the Copernican case was made to look stronger, and the argument of the Pope's favourite position put into the mouth of a character called Simplicio. The book had been licensed, but it enraged Pope Urban VIII, who had been Galileo's friend and admirer, and the Inquisition summoned the author to Rome in 1632.

## In the courtroom

Galileo, 69 and in poor health, reached Rome early in 1633. The examination turned on whether he had broken the 1616 injunction and on the way the book had been authorised. His defence was that he had never meant to defend the theory but to examine both sides. On 22 June he was sentenced, at the Dominican convent of Santa Maria sopra Minerva, to formal imprisonment and the Dialogue was banned. He read an abjuration in which he cursed and detested his error.

## Aftermath

The sentence was commuted to confinement at his villa at Arcetri near Florence, where he went blind and wrote the Two New Sciences. He died there in 1642. The legend that he muttered "and yet it moves" at the end of the trial has no contemporary source. In 1992 Pope John Paul II said that the theologians of the time had made mistakes, after a commission appointed in 1981 reported.
