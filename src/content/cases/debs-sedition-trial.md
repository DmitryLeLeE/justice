## The case

Eugene Debs had been the Socialist candidate for president four times and was the best-known labour leader in the country. After the United States entered the First World War in 1917, Congress passed the Espionage Act and, in 1918, amendments that made it a crime to speak against the war effort. On 16 June 1918 Debs spoke for two hours to a crowd at a park in Canton, Ohio, praising socialists who had been imprisoned for opposing the draft, and was arrested two weeks later.

## In the courtroom

The trial was in Cleveland in September before Judge David Westenhaver. The government's evidence was a transcript of the speech, made by stenographers. Debs's lawyers did not call witnesses, and Debs himself made a statement to the court after the jury found him guilty, in which he said that he stood with the oppressed. On 14 September 1918 he was sentenced to ten years.

## Aftermath

In March 1919 the Supreme Court unanimously upheld the conviction in an opinion by Justice Oliver Wendell Holmes. Debs entered the federal penitentiary in Atlanta in April 1919 and, as Prisoner 9653, ran for president in 1920, winning more than 900,000 votes. President Warren Harding commuted his sentence at Christmas 1921, and he died in 1926.
