## The case

In December 1919 Charles Ponzi started the Securities Exchange Company in Boston. He told investors that he could make 50 per cent in 45 days by buying international postal reply coupons cheaply abroad and exchanging them for stamps at a higher value elsewhere, a trade that was possible in theory but on a tiny scale. In reality the profits paid to the first investors came from money paid in by later ones. By the summer of 1920 thousands of people were queuing at his office and he was taking in vast sums.

## In the courtroom

The Boston Post, and then the financial analyst Clarence Barron, pointed out that there were not enough coupons in circulation to cover the money, and an audit showed that the company was insolvent. Ponzi was arrested on 12 August 1920, and in November pleaded guilty in federal court to using the mails to defraud. He was sentenced to five years. A Massachusetts jury convicted him of larceny in 1922.

## Aftermath

Ponzi served his federal term, and then a state sentence, and was deported in 1934. He died in a charity ward in Rio de Janeiro in January 1949. Investors in his scheme recovered a little more than a third of what they had put in.
