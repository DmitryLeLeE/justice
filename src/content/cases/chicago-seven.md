## The case

After the violent clashes between demonstrators and police at the 1968 Democratic National Convention, the federal government indicted a group of protest leaders on conspiracy and riot charges. The defendants were first known as the Chicago Eight.

The trial before Judge Julius Hoffman ran from September 1969. Black Panther chairman Bobby Seale was bound and gagged in court after repeated clashes, and his case was separated from the others. The remaining seven were tried as the Chicago Seven, and the trial became a national spectacle.

## In the courtroom

Howard Brodie drew the court for the whole trial, and his sketches are among the best-known images of it: Judge Hoffman, the lawyers, the gagged Seale. The drawing shown here, of Seale, comes from Commons without an artist's name. The judge was widely seen to be hostile to the defence. He ordered Seale chained and gagged, jailed Kunstler and the defendants for contempt, and was later criticised by the appeals court.

The defendants treated the courtroom as a stage. They wore judicial robes one day, brought a Viet Cong flag another, and spoke from the table. The result was less a trial of what happened at the convention than of the culture of the late 1960s.

## Aftermath

The jury acquitted all seven of conspiracy. Five were convicted of the lesser charge, and in 1972 the Seventh Circuit overturned those convictions, citing the judge's conduct. The contempt sentences, more than a hundred counts in all, were also set aside in later proceedings.
