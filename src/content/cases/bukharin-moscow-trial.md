## The case

The Moscow show trials of 1936 to 1938 were staged by Stalin's regime to destroy the leaders of the old Bolshevik party and its opposition. The last, in March 1938, was called the trial of the "Bloc of Rights and Trotskyites". Its main defendants were Nikolai Bukharin, who had been editor of Pravda and a Politburo member, Alexei Rykov, a former head of the government, and Genrikh Yagoda, the former chief of the secret police. They were accused of conspiring with foreign powers and with Leon Trotsky to overthrow the government, of sabotage, and of murders, including those of Sergei Kirov and the writer Maxim Gorky.

## In the courtroom

The trial before Vasily Ulrikh opened on 2 March 1938 in the October Hall of the House of Unions. The prosecutor, Andrei Vyshinsky, led the questioning. The defendants had been held for months and questioned hard, and nearly all confessed in court. Nikolai Krestinsky retracted his confession on the first day, saying that it was false, and withdrew the retraction the next. Bukharin admitted a general political responsibility but denied the specific charges of treason and murder, in a manner that many historians read as a disguised protest.

On 13 March the court sentenced eighteen defendants to death and three to terms of 15 to 25 years.

## Aftermath

The eighteen were shot on 15 March 1938. The three who got prison terms were killed later. In 1988, under Gorbachev, the Supreme Court of the Soviet Union rehabilitated Bukharin and most of his co-defendants, finding that there was no evidence of the crimes they had confessed to.

## 18+ · How the confessions were obtained

The defendants had been held in the NKVD's prisons for months. Records released after 1991 and later testimony indicate that they were questioned for long periods without sleep, threatened with harm to their families and, in some cases, physically abused. Yagoda, who had himself run the secret police, was among those accused. Bukharin wrote letters from his cell to Stalin and to the leadership professing his innocence, and his wife, Anna Larina, memorised a last message to the party that she delivered decades later.
