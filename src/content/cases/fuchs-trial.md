## The case

Klaus Fuchs fled Nazi Germany in 1933 and became a British citizen in 1942. He worked on the British atomic project and then, in 1944, went to the United States with the British mission to the Manhattan Project at Los Alamos, where he contributed to the plutonium bomb design. He had been passing information to Soviet intelligence since 1941. American code-breakers, working on the Venona decrypts, found traces of a spy with details that pointed to him, and in January 1950 he confessed to William Skardon, a British counter-intelligence interrogator.

## In the courtroom

The trial was short. Fuchs pleaded guilty on 1 March 1950 and the whole hearing took about ninety minutes. Attorney General Hartley Shawcross described the crime as one that exposed his country to great danger, and Lord Goddard, the Lord Chief Justice, sentenced him to 14 years, the maximum, on the ground that the offence was not treason because Britain and the Soviet Union had not been at war.

## Aftermath

Fuchs's confession led the FBI to Harry Gold, who led to David Greenglass and to the Rosenbergs. He was stripped of his British citizenship, was released after nine years and moved to East Germany, where he worked as a scientific director at the nuclear research institute at Rossendorf until 1979.
