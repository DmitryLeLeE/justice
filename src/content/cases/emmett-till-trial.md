## The case

Emmett Till, 14, travelled from Chicago to visit relatives in Money, Mississippi, in August 1955. After he was accused of offending Carolyn Bryant, the white woman who ran a store, her husband Roy Bryant and his half-brother J. W. Milam took him from his great-uncle's house in the early hours of 28 August. His body was found three days later in the Tallahatchie River, and his great-uncle Mose Wright identified it.

His mother, Mamie Till-Bradley, insisted on an open casket at the funeral in Chicago, and tens of thousands filed past. Photographs of his face were published in the Black press and seen across the country.

## In the courtroom

The trial took place in September 1955 in the Tallahatchie County courthouse in Sumner. The jury was all white and all male, since women and Black residents did not sit on juries in the county. Mose Wright, who risked his life by doing so, stood up in court and pointed out the two defendants as the men who took the boy. The defence argued that the body could not be positively identified and suggested that Till might be alive. Black reporters and visitors were seated at a separate table, and the case was covered by the national press.

The jury deliberated for 67 minutes and acquitted both men. In November a grand jury declined to indict them for kidnapping.

## Aftermath

In 1956 Bryant and Milam, protected from a second trial by double jeopardy, described the killing to the journalist William Bradford Huie for Look magazine and were paid for it. The verdict became a rallying point for the movement that followed within months, including the Montgomery bus boycott. In 2022 an unserved 1955 warrant naming Carolyn Bryant was found in a courthouse basement, and a grand jury declined to indict her.

## 18+ · What was done to him

Till was beaten, shot in the head, and the body weighted with a cotton-gin fan tied around the neck with barbed wire before it was put into the river. When it was recovered the face was so damaged that Wright could identify it only by a ring. The decision to show it to the public is what turned a local crime into a national event.
