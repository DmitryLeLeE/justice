## The case

Dred Scott had been born enslaved in Virginia and was taken by his owner, an army surgeon, to the free state of Illinois and to a fort in what is now Minnesota, then free territory under the Missouri Compromise. After the surgeon's death, Scott and his wife Harriet sued in a St. Louis court in 1846 on the grounds that they had become free by living on free soil. A jury ruled for them, the Missouri Supreme Court reversed the decision, and the case moved to the federal courts and then to the Supreme Court.

## In the courtroom

The Court decided the case in March 1857. Chief Justice Roger Taney's opinion held that people of African descent, whether enslaved or free, were not and could never be citizens of the United States and so could not sue in federal court. He added that the Missouri Compromise, which had banned slavery north of a certain line, was unconstitutional, because Congress had no power to bar slaveholders from taking their property into the territories. Justices Benjamin Curtis and John McLean dissented at length.

## Aftermath

The decision outraged Northerners and helped the new Republican Party, and it was cited in the Lincoln–Douglas debates of 1858. Scott's owners freed him soon after the ruling. He died of tuberculosis in 1858. The Civil War ended with the Thirteenth Amendment, and the Fourteenth in 1868 made all persons born in the United States citizens. Taney's opinion is generally regarded as one of the worst in the Court's history.
