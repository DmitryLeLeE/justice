## The case

General Tomoyuki Yamashita took command of Japanese forces in the Philippines in October 1944. During the American reconquest, Japanese troops in Manila and elsewhere killed large numbers of civilians and prisoners. Yamashita surrendered in September 1945 and was charged by the US Army with having failed, as the commander, to control his troops and prevent those crimes.

## In the courtroom

The trial opened in Manila on 29 October 1945 before a commission of five American generals, and lasted until 7 December. The prosecution did not claim that Yamashita had ordered the atrocities; the charges named the Manila massacre, the killing of 139 American prisoners at Palawan, and many others. His chief counsel, Colonel Harry Clarke, argued that he was being tried for losing the war, not for a crime he committed, and that communications had broken down so that he could not have controlled his men. The commission found him guilty and sentenced him to death.

## Aftermath

General MacArthur confirmed the sentence, the Supreme Court of the Philippines and the Supreme Court of the United States declined to interfere, and Justice Wiley Rutledge dissented. Yamashita was hanged on 23 February 1946 at Los Baños. The ruling, cited as In re Yamashita, gave its name to the "Yamashita standard": a commander is responsible for war crimes of subordinates if he did not try to find out about them and stop them. The principle later influenced the war crimes tribunals for the former Yugoslavia and the International Criminal Court.
