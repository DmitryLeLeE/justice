## The case

In March 1925 Tennessee passed the Butler Act, banning the teaching in public schools of any doctrine that denied the biblical account of human creation. The American Civil Liberties Union offered to pay for the defence of anyone charged under it. Townspeople in Dayton agreed, and on 25 May 1925 the schoolteacher John Scopes was charged. The trial was meant to put the small town on the map.

Hearings ran from 10 to 21 July. The prosecution was backed by Bryan, a former presidential candidate; the defence was led by the lawyer Darrow. Judge Raulston refused to hear scientific experts. So on 20 July Darrow called Bryan himself to the stand and questioned him as an authority on the Bible. The next day the judge struck that examination from the record.

The jury deliberated for about ten minutes. In 1927 the state Supreme Court upheld the law's constitutionality but set aside the verdict: under state law the jury, not the judge, should have set the fine.

## In the courtroom

The case was staged. George Rappleyea, a local mining manager, persuaded Dayton's leading citizens that a test case would bring the town attention, and Scopes agreed to be the defendant. The American Civil Liberties Union had offered to defend any teacher charged under the law. Both sides brought national figures to a county courthouse in a town of a few thousand people.

The judge would not let the defence call scientific experts, and their statements were read into the record instead. The heat and the crowds led Raulston to move the court outdoors on 20 July, and that afternoon the defence called Bryan as an expert on the Bible. Darrow asked him about Jonah, the age of the earth and the creation days. Bryan, who had come to Dayton to argue the prosecution's case, was forced to concede points about literal interpretation. Raulston ended the exchange and struck it from the record the following day.

## Aftermath

With the examination gone, the defence asked for a guilty verdict so that it could appeal. The jury came back in minutes, and Scopes was fined $100. Bryan died in Dayton on 26 July. In 1927 the Tennessee Supreme Court upheld the law but set aside the fine because the judge, not the jury, had imposed it, and it urged the state not to retry the case. The Butler Act stayed on the books until 1967.
