## The case

The hearings ran from 20 November 1945 to 1 October 1946 in the Nuremberg Palace of Justice. The building had survived the bombing largely intact and had a prison attached, which is why it was chosen. Judges and prosecutors came from the USSR, the United States, Britain and France.

Of 24 men indicted, 22 were tried. Robert Ley killed himself before the trial began, and Gustav Krupp was found medically unfit to stand trial. Martin Bormann was tried in absentia.

Ten death sentences were carried out on 16 October 1946; Hermann Göring killed himself the day before his execution. The trial established the principle that individuals, including heads of state, are answerable for crimes under international law.

## In the courtroom

The tribunal worked in four languages at once, using a system of simultaneous interpretation that had not been used in a court before. The prosecution built much of its case on the Nazis' own paperwork and showed film shot in the camps. The defendants, Göring above all, tried to turn the cross-examination into a contest, and Jackson's cross-examination of him is remembered as one that went badly for the prosecution.

Ed Vebell's drawing, made for a magazine, shows the front row of the dock as the court saw it.

## Aftermath

Twelve men were sentenced to death, three to life, and four to terms of 10 to 20 years. Three were acquitted: Schacht, von Papen and Fritzsche. The USSR judge dissented on the acquittals. Göring killed himself in his cell on the eve of the hangings, and the ten bodies were cremated and the ashes scattered. Twelve further trials followed at Nuremberg under American authority from 1946 to 1949. The Charter's principles were affirmed by the UN General Assembly in 1946.
