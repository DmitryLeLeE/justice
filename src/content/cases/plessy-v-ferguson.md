## The case

In 1890 Louisiana required railway companies to provide separate cars for white and Black passengers. A group of Black, Creole and white citizens in New Orleans, the Citizens' Committee, decided to challenge it, with the support of the railway company, which disliked the cost. Homer Plessy, a shoemaker who was seven-eighths white by ancestry but counted as Black under Louisiana law, bought a first-class ticket on 7 June 1892, sat in the white car, and refused to move. A detective, who had been told in advance, arrested him.

## In the courtroom

Judge John Howard Ferguson of the New Orleans criminal court rejected Plessy's argument that the law violated the Fourteenth Amendment, and the Louisiana Supreme Court agreed. His lawyer, the white novelist and former judge Albion Tourgée, took the case to Washington. In May 1896 Justice Henry Billings Brown wrote for seven of the eight justices (one did not take part) that segregation did not make Black people inferior unless they chose to see it so, and that equal but separate facilities were constitutional. Justice John Marshall Harlan dissented alone, writing that the Constitution is colour-blind.

## Aftermath

"Separate but equal" became the legal basis of segregated schools, transport and public places throughout the South. The Supreme Court overruled it for public education in Brown v. Board of Education on 17 May 1954 and then dismantled it in other fields.
