## The case

The military dictatorship took power in March 1976. After democracy returned, President Raúl Alfonsín demanded that the junta leaders be tried. Hearings in case no. 13/84 opened on 22 April 1985 and ended with the verdict on 9 December.

More than 800 witnesses appeared in the courtroom. Prosecutor Julio César Strassera's closing speech, with its appeal "Nunca más" ("Never again"), entered the history of the trial. The court found that the juntas devised and carried out a criminal plan to fight "subversion", leaving officers wide latitude in arrests and killings.

The convicted men were later pardoned, and in 2007 a federal court declared Videla's pardon invalid. The story of the trial inspired the film "Argentina, 1985", nominated for the Academy Award for Best International Feature.

## In the courtroom

The first trial was hard-fought. The armed forces' own court had refused to try its leaders, and the case passed to the civilian Cámara Federal. The prosecutors, young and with little time, chose a representative set of cases from thousands of complaints. They put on the stand survivors of the clandestine detention centres, relatives of the disappeared, and a few former officials.

The testimony reached the public through a weekly newspaper and short television clips. When Strassera finished his summing-up he ended with two words that became a national slogan, "Nunca más", taken from the title of the report of the national commission on the disappeared.

## Verdict

The court found that the juntas had planned and carried out systematic kidnapping, torture and murder. Videla and Massera were sentenced to life imprisonment; Viola received 17 years, Lambruschini 8 and Agosti 4 and a half. Four other defendants were acquitted. The prosecution had asked for life terms for five.

## Aftermath

The military rebelled in the next years, and Alfonsín's government passed the Full Stop and Due Obedience laws that halted further prosecutions. Menem pardoned the junta leaders in 1989 and 1990. Congress annulled the amnesty laws in 2003, and later trials sent hundreds of officers to prison. Videla died in prison in 2013.
