## The case

President Lincoln was shot at Ford's Theatre on 14 April 1865 and died the next morning. In the weeks that followed, authorities arrested a group of people accused of helping John Wilkes Booth plan the attack. They were tried not in a civilian court but by a military commission, which sat in the Old Arsenal Penitentiary in Washington in May and June 1865.

The courtroom was drawn by artists for Harper's Weekly and other illustrated papers, and their engravings gave the public its view of the proceedings. Four of the defendants were hanged at the penitentiary on 7 July 1865.

Whether civilians could be tried by a military commission was disputed at the time and has been argued about since.

## In the courtroom

The commission heard more than 300 witnesses in seven weeks. The defendants sat on a raised platform, and the four who were to be hanged, Mary Surratt, Powell, Herold and Atzerodt, were sentenced on 30 June. Dr. Samuel Mudd, who had set Booth's broken leg, Arnold and O'Laughlen received life imprisonment and Spangler six years. Mary Surratt became the first woman executed by the federal government.

The defendants, who were hooded in the cells, could not testify in their own defence under the rules of the time.

## Aftermath

Several members of the commission asked for mercy for Surratt, but President Johnson did not see the petition, or said that he did not. In 1866 the Supreme Court held in Ex parte Milligan that civilians could not be tried by military tribunals where the civil courts were open. Mudd and the others were pardoned in 1869. John Surratt, who had fled, was tried in a civilian court in 1867 and the jury could not agree.
