## The case

On 25 March 1931 a posse stopped a freight train at Paint Rock, Alabama, after a fight between white and Black youths who were riding it. Two white women on the train, Victoria Price and Ruby Bates, told the posse that they had been raped by nine of the Black teenagers. A doctor who examined the women found no evidence of rape. The nine, aged from 13 to 19, were held in Scottsboro under guard against a crowd that threatened lynching.

## In the courtroom

The trials took place within weeks, before an all-white jury. The defendants had no lawyer of their own until the trial day, when the court named local counsel who had not prepared. Judge Hawkins presided; eight of the boys were sentenced to death, and the trial of 14-year-old Roy Wright ended in a hung jury.

In 1932 the US Supreme Court, in Powell v. Alabama, ruled that the denial of effective counsel in a capital case violated due process. At the new trials in Decatur the New York lawyer Samuel Leibowitz defended them, and Ruby Bates changed her story and testified that no rape had occurred. Judge James Horton, in 1933, set aside the guilty verdict on Haywood Patterson because the evidence did not support it, and was voted out of office the next year. In Norris v. Alabama in 1935 the Supreme Court held that excluding Black people from jury rolls was unconstitutional.

## Aftermath

Alabama retried four of the men again and convicted them, with sentences from 75 years to death; in 1937 the state dropped the charges against four others. The men served years in prison, Patterson escaped in 1948, and Clarence Norris, the last survivor, died in 1989. In 2013 the Alabama pardon board granted posthumous pardons to Weems, Wright and Patterson. The case became a landmark in the civil rights movement and in the law on counsel and juries.
