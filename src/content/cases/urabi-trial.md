## The case

Ahmed Urabi, an Egyptian army colonel from a peasant family, led a nationalist movement in 1881 and 1882 against the rule of Khedive Tewfik and against European control of Egypt's finances. In 1882 Britain bombarded Alexandria and sent an army under Sir Garnet Wolseley, which defeated Urabi's forces at Tel el-Kebir on 13 September. Cairo surrendered without a fight, and Urabi and the other nationalist leaders were captured.

## In the courtroom

The restored Khedive tried Urabi for rebellion before an Egyptian court-martial in Cairo. The British, who now held the country, did not want him to be hanged, and the Khedive wanted his death. Two British lawyers, Richard Eve and Alexander Meyrick Broadley, were hired for the defence, and an understanding was reached through Lord Dufferin under which Urabi would plead guilty and the death sentence be commuted. He did so on 3 December 1882, and the sentence was at once changed to banishment for life.

## Aftermath

Urabi left Egypt for Ceylon on 28 December 1882 and lived there for nearly twenty years, supporting education for Muslims. Khedive Abbas II allowed him to come home in 1901, and he died in Cairo in 1911, aged 70. The revolt gave Britain the pretext for an occupation of Egypt that lasted for decades, while Urabi came to be remembered by many Egyptians as an early national hero.
