## The case

William Cosby, the royal governor of New York, had made enemies by dismissing a chief justice who ruled against him. His opponents financed a newspaper, the New York Weekly Journal, which was printed by John Peter Zenger, a German-born printer, and which attacked the governor and his officials in sharp terms. In November 1734 Cosby had Zenger arrested for seditious libel, and he stayed in jail for months, though his wife Anna kept the paper going.

## In the courtroom

At the time, the truth of a published statement was not a defence to libel: under English law the offence lay in the effect of the words on public order, and the judge, not the jury, decided whether they were libellous. Zenger's own lawyers were disbarred by the court, and Andrew Hamilton, an eminent lawyer from Philadelphia, took over at the trial in August 1735. He admitted that Zenger had printed the articles and argued that, if the statements were true, there was no libel. Chief Justice DeLancey told the jury that the truth was no defence, but the jurors acquitted after a short deliberation.

## Aftermath

The verdict set no formal precedent, but newspapers and later writers treated it as a founding example of the free press. Truth later became a defence to libel in American law, and the case is cited in discussions of the First Amendment.
