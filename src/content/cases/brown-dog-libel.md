## The case

In 1903 the anti-vivisection campaigner Stephen Coleridge published a claim that the physiologist William Bayliss had operated on a conscious brown terrier at University College London in front of a lecture audience. Bayliss sued for libel.

The trial took place in November 1903 and was covered by the illustrated press. The jury sided with Bayliss. The affair did not end there: a bronze memorial to the dog erected in Battersea in 1906 became the target of protests.

## In the courtroom

William Bayliss, a respected physiologist, sued after Coleridge said in a speech that Bayliss had cut a conscious brown terrier. The trial turned on a plain question: had the dog been anaesthetised? Bayliss produced the laboratory staff and the licence that allowed the experiment. The Lord Chief Justice's summing-up leaned toward the scientist.

## Aftermath

The anti-vivisectionists lost in court and raised the money for Bayliss's damages themselves. They then put up a bronze statue of the brown dog in Battersea, with an inscription about the case. Medical students attacked it for years, and the "Brown Dog riots" of 1907 pulled in police and trade unionists. The statue was taken down in 1910 and a new one went up in 1985.
