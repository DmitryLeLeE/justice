## The case

Cora Crippen, a music-hall singer who performed as Belle Elmore, disappeared in January 1910. Her husband, an American-born doctor who sold patent medicines in London, told friends that she had gone to America and later that she had died there. Suspicious friends went to Scotland Yard, and Chief Inspector Walter Dew searched the house at 39 Hilldrop Crescent twice. On the second search, in July, he found human remains buried under the cellar floor. By then Crippen had fled with his secretary and lover, Ethel Le Neve, who was dressed as a boy.

The captain of the liner Montrose, Henry Kendall, noticed the odd couple and sent a wireless message to Britain. Dew boarded a faster ship, reached Canada first, and arrested Crippen on the Montrose near Father Point on 31 July. It was one of the first times that wireless telegraphy was used to catch a fugitive.

## In the courtroom

The trial before Lord Alverstone at the Old Bailey lasted four days, in October. The prosecution, led by Richard Muir, relied on the remains and on the pathologist Bernard Spilsbury, who said that a piece of skin with a scar came from Cora Crippen's abdomen and that the remains contained the poison hyoscine, which Crippen had bought shortly before she vanished. Crippen did not deny that the remains were found in his cellar; he said that he knew nothing of them. The jury needed under half an hour to find him guilty.

## Aftermath

Crippen was hanged at Pentonville prison on 23 November 1910, still saying that he was innocent. Ethel Le Neve was tried separately as an accessory and acquitted. A DNA study published in 2010 said that the remains did not come from Cora's family line; other scientists have questioned that finding and the case is still argued over.
