## The case

The 28 defendants were indicted on 29 April 1946 and hearings began on 3 May in the former War Ministry building in Tokyo's Ichigaya district. The tribunal had 11 judges from the Allied nations.

During the trial two defendants died and another was found unfit to take part because of mental illness. Sentences were read in November 1948 and the executions took place in December at Sugamo Prison.

The decision was not unanimous. Radhabinod Pal held that the charges of waging aggressive war lacked a sufficient basis in the international law of the time, and he voted to acquit every defendant on every count. Whether the tribunal delivered justice or victors' vengeance is still argued.

## In the courtroom

The tribunal sat for two and a half years, longer than the Nuremberg trial, and its records fill tens of thousands of pages. It heard evidence of the invasion of Manchuria and China, the Nanjing massacre, the treatment of prisoners of war and the road to Pearl Harbor. Emperor Hirohito was never charged, a decision taken by the Allied command in Tokyo and Washington.

Judges came from eleven countries, among them Webb of Australia, Mei Ju-ao of China and Radhabinod Pal of India. Webb's court convicted all of the defendants who lived to hear the judgment: seven were sentenced to death, sixteen to life imprisonment, and two to shorter terms. Three men did not reach the verdict: Matsuoka and Nagano died during the trial and Okawa was judged unfit.

## Dissents

Pal wrote a full dissent that would have acquitted every defendant. Bernard of France and Röling of the Netherlands also filed separate opinions. The dissents were not read in court and were only published later.

## Aftermath

The hanged men included Tojo and Hirota, the only civilian executed. The remaining prisoners were released in the 1950s on parole, and some returned to political life. The tribunal's legitimacy remains disputed in Japan and across Asia.
