## The case

Mary Phagan, a 13-year-old worker at an Atlanta pencil factory, was found dead in April 1913. Leo Frank, the factory's superintendent, was charged with her murder.

The trial opened at the end of July 1913 in a courtroom packed with spectators, with crowds outside. The prosecution's case rested heavily on the testimony of a factory worker, Jim Conley, and Frank was convicted.

Appeals failed, but Governor John Slaton reviewed the evidence and commuted the sentence in June 1915. In August 1915 a group of men took Frank from prison and lynched him. In 1986 the Georgia Board of Pardons and Paroles granted him a posthumous pardon, without ruling on his guilt.

## In the courtroom

The case against Frank, a Jewish factory manager from the North in a Southern city, rested on the sworn account of Jim Conley, the Black sweeper who had been seen with a note found beside the body. Conley changed his story several times. Crowds outside the courthouse shouted at the jury, and the verdict was heard through the windows.

The newspaper sketch shows the first day, with the testimony of Newt Lee, the night watchman who found the body.

## Afterwards

In 1982 a witness, Alonzo Mann, who had been an office boy at the factory, said he had seen Conley with the body. The 1986 pardon avoided the question of guilt. The lynching was followed by the revival of the Ku Klux Klan on Stone Mountain that autumn, and in 1913 the Anti-Defamation League had been founded in response to the trial. The case is a standard text on prejudice and the press.
