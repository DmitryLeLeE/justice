## The case

In April 1895 Oscar Wilde brought a criminal libel case against the Marquess of Queensberry, who had accused him of posing as a sodomite. The case collapsed when Queensberry's lawyers produced evidence about Wilde's private life, and Wilde was arrested.

The criminal trials took place at the Old Bailey. The first jury failed to reach a verdict; at the retrial he was found guilty under the law against "gross indecency" between men and sentenced to two years' hard labour.

The Illustrated Police News printed drawings of the proceedings, among them the page used here.

## In the courtroom

The disaster began with the libel case. When Wilde was cross-examined by Edward Carson on his friendships with young working-class men, his withdrawal of the action was a certain prelude to his arrest. At the criminal trials the case against him rested on the accounts of the young men, men who had been introduced to Wilde and some of whom had earlier tried to blackmail him.

At the second trial the jury convicted, and Mr Justice Wills called it the worst case he had ever tried and gave the maximum, two years with hard labour.

## Aftermath

Wilde served his sentence at Pentonville, Wandsworth and Reading, and he wrote "De Profundis" in prison. After his release he left England for good. He and thousands of other men convicted under the same law were pardoned posthumously in 2017.
