## The case

Fritz Haarmann was arrested in Hanover in June 1924 after human remains were found in the river Leine. He was accused of killing a large number of young men and boys in the years after the First World War.

The trial opened in Hanover on 4 December 1924. The archive record of the drawing shown here, from Amsterdam's city archive, describes it as made during the trial of the "mass murderer Haarmann", who was suspected of killing 27 young people. The court sentenced him to death.

## In the courtroom

Haarmann was a petty criminal and police informer in postwar Hanover who preyed on runaways and rootless young men at the railway station. He told the court, with startling calm, about killings he claimed not to remember in detail. The police had failed for years to act on missing-persons reports, and in the trial it became clear that some of them had known him as an informant.

The drawing reproduced here comes from the Amsterdam city archive and was made during the trial; the killer is on the left.

## Aftermath

Haarmann was beheaded in April 1925. His accomplice Hans Grans was also sentenced to death, and the sentence was later reduced. The case drew attention to the disorder of the Weimar years, and to a police force that had let a killer work for six years.

## 18+ · The victims

Haarmann was convicted of 24 murders of boys and young men, between about 1918 and 1924, though he was charged with 27 and the true number is unknown. Many were runaways or homeless, picked up at Hanover railway station. By his own confession he killed them by biting through the throat, then took the bodies apart in his flat and threw the remains into the river Leine. Human skulls found on its banks in 1924 started the investigation, and rumours that he had sold the flesh as meat were widespread at the time, though the court never settled them.
