## The case

On 15 April 1920 two men carrying a factory payroll, the paymaster Frederick Parmenter and the guard Alessandro Berardelli, were shot dead in South Braintree, Massachusetts, and the robbers escaped in a car. Three weeks later the police arrested Nicola Sacco, a shoemaker, and Bartolomeo Vanzetti, a fish peddler, both Italian immigrants and anarchists, who were carrying guns when they were stopped. In the middle of the first Red Scare, with bombings and deportations of radicals in the news, their politics weighed heavily.

## In the courtroom

The trial before Judge Webster Thayer began on 31 May 1921 and lasted six weeks. The prosecution relied on eyewitnesses who disagreed on what they had seen, and on ballistics evidence that was disputed. Both men said that they had lied to the police about their politics because they feared deportation, and each offered an alibi. The jury found them guilty on 14 July. Judge Thayer was reported to have used abusive language about the defendants outside the courtroom, and motions for a new trial, including those based on the confession of another man, were refused by him.

## Aftermath

A committee appointed by Governor Alvan Fuller reviewed the case and upheld the verdict. Protests took place in Europe and Latin America, and the two men were executed in the electric chair on 23 August 1927. Later ballistics tests have been read both ways, and historians still disagree over whether either of them was guilty. In 1977 Governor Michael Dukakis declared that the trial and the review had been marked by prejudice and that no stigma should attach to their names.
