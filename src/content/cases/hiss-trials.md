## The case

On 3 August 1948 Whittaker Chambers, an editor at Time and a former Communist Party member, told the House Un-American Activities Committee that Alger Hiss, a former senior State Department official, had belonged to a secret Communist network in Washington in the 1930s. Hiss denied it under oath and sued Chambers for slander. In response Chambers produced typed copies of State Department documents, and on 2 December he led investigators to a hollowed-out pumpkin on his Maryland farm and took out rolls of microfilm. Because the statute of limitations on spying had expired, Hiss was indicted for perjury.

## In the courtroom

The first trial began on 31 May 1949 before Judge Samuel Kaufman and ended on 7 July with a hung jury, split eight to four for conviction. At the second, before Judge Henry Goddard, the prosecutor Thomas Murphy relied on the typed copies, which the FBI matched to a Woodstock typewriter once owned by the Hisses. The defence introduced another Woodstock machine, and later writers have argued over whether it was a fake. The jury found Hiss guilty on 21 January 1950, and on 25 January he was given five years.

## Aftermath

Hiss was released on 27 November 1954 after serving three years and eight months, and spent the rest of his life asserting his innocence. Decrypted Soviet cables published in 1995 by the Venona project included a spy code-named "Ales", whom an FBI analyst believed to be Hiss; others call that evidence persuasive but not conclusive. He died in 1996 at 92.
