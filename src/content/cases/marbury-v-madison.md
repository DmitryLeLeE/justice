## The case

In the last hours of John Adams's presidency in March 1801, the outgoing Federalists filled a large number of new judicial posts. William Marbury, a Maryland businessman, was named a justice of the peace in the District of Columbia, but his commission was not delivered before Thomas Jefferson took office. Jefferson told his Secretary of State, James Madison, to withhold it. Marbury petitioned the Supreme Court to order Madison to hand it over.

## In the courtroom

The new Chief Justice, John Marshall, had himself been Adams's Secretary of State and had signed and sealed the commissions that were still undelivered. The Court, in an opinion by Marshall, held first that Marbury had a right to the commission and that withholding it was unlawful. But it said that the Court had no power to issue the order, because section 13 of the Judiciary Act of 1789, which seemed to grant it, went beyond what Article III of the Constitution allowed. Since the Constitution is the supreme law, a statute that conflicts with it is void, and it is the duty of the courts to say so.

## Aftermath

Marbury never got his commission. The Jefferson administration could not complain of the result, since it had won. The principle of judicial review, which the decision established, has become the basis of constitutional adjudication in the United States and has been copied in other countries.
