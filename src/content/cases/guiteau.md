## The case

Charles Guiteau shot President James Garfield at a Washington railway station on 2 July 1881. Garfield died on 19 September, and the trial opened that November.

Guiteau's lawyers argued that he was insane. Guiteau himself behaved erratically in court, interrupting witnesses and addressing the judge. The trial was reported around the world, and the illustrated press, including European weeklies, printed scenes of the courtroom.

The jury rejected the insanity defence and found him guilty. He was hanged in June 1882.

## In the courtroom

Guiteau was a disappointed office-seeker who believed that God had told him to remove the president. The trial lasted eleven weeks and was one of the first American trials in which medical experts publicly argued over insanity. Guiteau himself kept interrupting, wrote and recited verse in the dock, and issued opinions on the case to the newspapers.

The defence took a second line which mattered: that Garfield had died of his doctors' care and not of the bullet. Guiteau said, "The doctors killed Garfield, I just shot him." It did not help.

## Aftermath

The jury took less than an hour, and the sentence was carried out on 30 June 1882. The assassination led to the Pendleton Civil Service Reform Act of 1883, which ended the spoils system in federal hiring. Guiteau's case also shaped the later law of insanity.
