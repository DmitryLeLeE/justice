## The case

Louis Riel had led the Métis provisional government at Red River in 1869 and 1870, and had then lived in exile, partly in the United States. In 1884 Métis communities in the Saskatchewan valley asked him to come back and press their claims to land. In March 1885 he proclaimed a provisional government, and fighting with the Canadian militia and the North-West Mounted Police followed. The Métis were defeated at Batoche in May, and Riel gave himself up a few days later.

## In the courtroom

The trial opened in Regina on 20 July 1885, not in Winnipeg as some had asked, before Judge Hugh Richardson and a jury of six, all English-speaking Protestants. The charge was high treason. Riel's lawyers wished to argue that he was insane and not responsible, but Riel rejected the plea and spoke to the jury in his own defence. The jury found him guilty and recommended mercy. On 1 August Richardson sentenced him to death.

## Aftermath

The federal government did not commute the sentence, and Riel was hanged in Regina on 16 November 1885. His execution deepened the split between French-speaking and English-speaking Canada, and in Quebec it left a long political mark. He has since been recognised as a founder of Manitoba, and historians still argue over whether he was a rebel, a visionary, or both.
