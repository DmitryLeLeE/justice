## The case

On 4 July 1954 Marilyn Sheppard, who was pregnant, was beaten to death in the bedroom of the family's house in Bay Village, a suburb of Cleveland. Her husband, Sam Sheppard, an osteopath, said that he had been asleep downstairs, heard her cry, and fought with an intruder who knocked him out. The police doubted his story, and Cleveland's newspapers, led by the Cleveland Press, demanded his arrest. One front-page editorial was headed "Why Isn't Sam Sheppard in Jail?".

## In the courtroom

The trial before Judge Edward Blythin opened in October 1954 in a courtroom that was crowded with reporters. They sat inside the bar, near the jury, and the judge did not shield the jurors from coverage. Sheppard was convicted of second-degree murder in December and sentenced to life.

In 1966, on a petition brought by a young Boston lawyer, F. Lee Bailey, the Supreme Court ruled in Sheppard v. Maxwell that the trial judge had failed to protect the defendant from prejudicial publicity. At the retrial that autumn the jury acquitted him.

## Aftermath

Sheppard never recovered his career and died in 1970. Later DNA evidence pointed to Richard Eberling, a window washer who had worked at the house, but a civil suit by Sheppard's son in 2000 found that the family could not prove that he had been wrongfully imprisoned. The case is the leading authority on how courts must handle press coverage, and loosely inspired the television series and film The Fugitive.
