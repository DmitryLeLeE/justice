## The case

After the war Eichmann hid in Argentina. In May 1960 Israeli intelligence agents seized him and took him to Israel. All counts rested on the 1950 law on the punishment of Nazis and their collaborators.

The hearings opened on 11 April 1961 in Beit Ha'am, a new community centre in central Jerusalem converted into a courtroom for several hundred spectators. The defendant sat in a glass booth. Eichmann said he had only followed orders.

Witness testimony ended in mid-August and the verdict was read in December. The philosopher Hannah Arendt wrote a book on the trial, "Eichmann in Jerusalem", and its conclusions are still debated.

## In the courtroom

Prosecutor Hausner chose to build the case on the testimony of survivors as well as on documents, so that the Holocaust was told in the voices of people who had lived through it. The sessions were filmed and shown abroad. Eichmann maintained that he was a transport official who had followed orders.

The judges refused that defence. They found that he had carried out the deportations with full knowledge and zeal. Defence counsel Servatius, paid by the Israeli state, argued unsuccessfully that Israel had no jurisdiction because Eichmann had been abducted and the crimes had taken place in Europe before the state existed.

## Aftermath

Eichmann was hanged in 1962, the only civil execution Israel has carried out. His ashes were scattered at sea outside Israeli waters. Arendt's reports from the trial for The New Yorker, published as a book in 1963, introduced the phrase "the banality of evil" and set off an argument among historians that has not ended.
