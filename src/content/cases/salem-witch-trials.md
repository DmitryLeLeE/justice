## The case

In the winter of 1692 several girls in Salem Village, in the Massachusetts Bay colony, began to suffer fits that local doctors blamed on witchcraft. They named women of the village, among them a slave, Tituba, and a beggar, Sarah Good. Accusations spread through the surrounding towns, and by the spring the jails held dozens of prisoners. The new governor, William Phips, set up a special court of Oyer and Terminer to hear the cases.

## In the courtroom

The court sat from June, with William Stoughton as chief justice. A good deal of the evidence was "spectral": witnesses said that the shape or spirit of the accused had tormented them, a kind of testimony that many ministers warned was unreliable. Those who confessed were spared, and those who maintained their innocence were hanged. The first to die, Bridget Bishop, was hanged on 10 June; in all, nineteen people were hanged between June and September, and an old farmer, Giles Corey, who would not enter a plea, was pressed under stones.

Increase Mather and other ministers argued that spectral evidence should not be accepted, and in October Phips dissolved the court. A new court, which refused to take spectral evidence, acquitted most of those it tried in 1693, and Phips pardoned the rest.

## Aftermath

Judge Samuel Sewall publicly apologised in 1697, and the colony held a day of fasting. In 1711 the legislature reversed the convictions of many of those condemned and paid compensation to families. Several more names were cleared in 1957 and 2001, and in 2022 Massachusetts exonerated the last, Elizabeth Johnson Jr. The trials remain the standard American example of mass hysteria and of the danger of unreliable evidence.
