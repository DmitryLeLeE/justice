## The case

After the Soviet Union tested an atomic bomb in 1949, the FBI traced leaks from the Los Alamos laboratory. Harry Gold, a courier, was arrested in May 1950 and named David Greenglass, an Army machinist at Los Alamos. Greenglass named his sister and brother-in-law, Ethel and Julius Rosenberg, and a grand jury indicted them on 17 August 1950.

## In the courtroom

The trial opened on 6 March 1951 under Judge Irving Kaufman. The prosecution team was led by Irving Saypol with Roy Cohn, and Emanuel Bloch defended both defendants. David Greenglass was the main witness: he said he had passed sketches of the bomb to Julius and that Ethel had typed his notes, and his wife Ruth supported him. Gold also testified. The Rosenbergs refused to answer questions about their politics, citing the Fifth Amendment.

The jury returned guilty verdicts on 29 March. On 5 April Kaufman sentenced both to death, saying their crime was worse than murder. Greenglass, who cooperated, got fifteen years, and Morton Sobell received a long prison term.

## Aftermath

Appeals and appeals for clemency, including one from Pope Pius XII, were refused. Justice William O. Douglas granted a stay that was quickly lifted. The Rosenbergs were electrocuted at Sing Sing on 19 June 1953, Julius first; doctors found that Ethel's heart was still beating after the first round of current and she was given additional shocks.

The Venona decrypts, released in 1995, showed Julius to have been a recruiter and courier for Soviet intelligence. In 2001 David Greenglass said that his wife, not Ethel, had typed the notes and that his testimony against his sister was false. The harsh sentence on Ethel remains controversial.
