## The case

D. H. Lawrence's novel had been published privately in 1928 and banned in Britain ever since. The Obscene Publications Act of 1959 allowed a defence of literary merit, and Penguin decided to test it by printing the whole book in a cheap paperback in 1960. The Director of Public Prosecutions brought a case against the firm.

## In the courtroom

The prosecutor, Mervyn Griffith-Jones, opened by asking the jury whether this was a book they would wish their wives or servants to read, a question that was taken as a sign of how far the court was from ordinary life. The defence called some thirty-five witnesses, including professors, writers and a bishop, who said that the novel had literary merit. The jury of nine men and three women acquitted.

The picture here is not a portrait of the proceedings but a seating plan of the courtroom, drawn by the Penguin designer Hans Schmoller.

## Aftermath

The decision opened the way for the publication of other books that had been suppressed. It is often placed at the start of a more permissive decade in British publishing.
