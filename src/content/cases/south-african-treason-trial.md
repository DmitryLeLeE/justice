## The case

In December 1956 the Security Branch arrested about 140 people across South Africa in a pre-dawn raid, and the number of accused grew to 156. They were Africans, Indians, coloured and white people from the African National Congress and allied organisations. The state said that the Freedom Charter, adopted at the Congress of the People at Kliptown in 1955, was a programme for a communist revolution, and that its signers had conspired to overthrow the government.

## In the courtroom

A preparatory examination ran at the Drill Hall in Johannesburg from December 1956 until January 1958, with crowds outside. In December 1957 the Attorney General dropped the charges against 61 of the accused, Albert Luthuli among them. The trial of the rest began in Pretoria in August 1958, with the defence led by Israel Maisels. In November 1958 the charges against 30 were reframed as a conspiracy, and in April 1959 Judge Rumpff ended the case against the other 61. After the Sharpeville shooting in March 1960 the ANC was banned and many of the defendants were detained under the state of emergency. On 29 March 1961 Rumpff found the remaining 28 not guilty, ruling that the ANC had not been shown to be communist and that violence had not been proved as its policy.

## Aftermath

The acquittal was a defeat for the state, but within months the ANC turned to armed struggle, and Nelson Mandela and others were tried again in the Rivonia trial of 1963 and 1964. A fund set up in London by Canon John Collins, the Defence and Aid Fund, paid the legal costs and supported families, and became a lasting source of money for opponents of apartheid.
