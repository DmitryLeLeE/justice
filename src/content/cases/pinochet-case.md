## The case

Augusto Pinochet seized power in Chile in a coup in September 1973 and ruled until 1990. The Rettig report concluded that 2,279 people were killed for political reasons, and the later Valech report found that 31,947 people were tortured. Pinochet gave up the presidency but stayed as commander in chief and then as senator for life, with immunity at home. In October 1998 he travelled to London for medical treatment. A Spanish judge, Baltasar Garzón, who was investigating the deaths of Spaniards in Chile and Argentina, asked for his extradition, and the British police arrested him in his clinic.

## In the courtroom

The case reached the House of Lords, then Britain's highest court, and was heard twice. The first ruling, in November 1998, rejected Pinochet's immunity, but it was set aside because one of the judges had links to Amnesty International. On 24 March 1999 a differently composed panel held that a former head of state has no immunity for torture committed after 1988, when Britain had joined the Convention against Torture. That narrowed the charges that could be heard, but allowed the extradition to go forward.

## Aftermath

Home Secretary Jack Straw decided in January 2000 that Pinochet was unfit for trial, and he was released on 2 March, returning to Chile the next day. In Chile the Supreme Court lifted his immunity in August 2000 and, after rulings in 2002 and 2004 about his fitness, allowed cases to proceed, but he died on 10 December 2006 without being convicted. The case showed that a former dictator could be arrested abroad, and it influenced human rights prosecutions in Latin America and elsewhere.
