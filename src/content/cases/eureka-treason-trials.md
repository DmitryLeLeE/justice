## The case

In 1854 the diggers of the Victorian goldfields, taxed by a licence fee that had to be paid whether or not a man found gold, and policed by troopers who made licence hunts, rose in protest at Ballarat. They built a stockade at Eureka, and at dawn on 3 December 1854 troops and police attacked it. It was over within minutes. Lalor's own list named 22 rebels dead, and the official toll was 27, including several soldiers. The government arrested many of the diggers and charged thirteen of them with high treason.

## In the courtroom

The trials took place in Melbourne in early 1855. The first accused was John Joseph, an African American digger who had been in the stockade, and he was acquitted. The lawyer Archibald Michie led the defence, and a young barrister, Butler Cole Aspinall, acted for Joseph without a fee. Melbourne juries, who shared the diggers' dislike of the licence, acquitted all thirteen, among them Raffaello Carboni, an Italian who later wrote a book about the rising.

## Aftermath

The government dropped the charges against the others, and the leader of the rebels, Peter Lalor, who had lost an arm in the attack, was elected to the Legislative Assembly later in 1855. The licence fee was replaced by a cheaper "miner's right" that also gave the vote, and from 1857 all adult men could vote in elections to the Assembly. Historians still argue about how much of the rising was about democracy and how much about taxes.
